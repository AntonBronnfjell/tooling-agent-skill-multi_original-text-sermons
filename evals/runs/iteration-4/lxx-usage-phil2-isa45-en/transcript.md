# Every Knee, Every Tongue — Philippians 2:10–11 and Isaiah 45:21–25

## Introduction

Most of us know Philippians 2:10–11 well enough to finish the sentence: "every knee should bow… every tongue confess." Fewer of us have read the older sentence those words echo. Paul does not footnote it, but the wording is close enough to Isaiah 45 that it is worth reading the two side by side, slowly, in the languages they were written in.

Tonight's goal is a skill more than a conclusion: to learn to put a New Testament line next to the Old Testament text it echoes — the Hebrew, and the Greek translation Paul's readers used — and to notice exactly what is shared, what is different, and what is left for us to weigh.

## Text

Paul is writing in the first century to the assembly in Philippi. After describing how Jesus emptied and humbled himself, he says that God gave him "the name above every name" (Phil 2:9), and then:

> [GRC Phil.2.10] ἵνα ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι Ἰησοῦ πᾶν γόνυ κάμψῃ ἐπουρανίων καὶ ἐπιγείων καὶ καταχθονίων,
> Literal: so-that in/at the name of-Jesus every knee may-bow of-heavenly-ones and of-earthly-ones and of-under-the-earth-ones
> Natural: so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven, on earth and under the earth,

> [GRC Phil.2.11] καὶ πᾶσα γλῶσσα ἐξομολογήσηται ὅτι κύριος Ἰησοῦς Χριστὸς εἰς δόξαν θεοῦ πατρός.
> Literal: and every tongue may-acknowledge that lord/Lord [is] Jesus Anointed, to glory of-God father
> Natural: and every tongue should openly acknowledge that Jesus the Anointed is lord / Lord, to the glory of God the father.

## Big Idea

Paul sets the homage that Isaiah's speaker claims for himself — every knee, every tongue — around the name of Jesus, and closes it "to the glory of God the father."

Two sets of words carry this: the shared phrase "every knee … every tongue", and the two names Paul keeps side by side in one sentence, κύριος Ἰησοῦς and θεοῦ πατρός.

## Point 1

**Who is speaking in Isaiah 45?**

Before we look at the knees and tongues, notice the speaker. In the Hebrew of verse 21 he names himself: "Was it not I, YHWH?" Then twice he says there is no other — no other god besides me, none but me. Verse 22 repeats it: "I am God, and there is no other." The Greek translation gives the same claim in its own words: "I am God, and there is no other besides me."

Two observations you can reuse. First, look at what changes between Hebrew and Greek. Where the Hebrew of verse 21 has the name יְהוָה, the Greek has ὁ θεός, "God". A translation is a witness to how its translators read the text, not a photocopy. Second, notice the invitation in verse 22: "Turn to me and be rescued, all the ends of the earth." The oath of verse 23 is not addressed to Israel alone; it is addressed to the whole earth.

The one Dead Sea Scroll that preserves the whole passage, the great Isaiah scroll (1QIsaa), reads almost letter for letter with the Masoretic text here; the differences are mostly spelling. A second scroll, 4Q56, preserves only scattered letters. The details are in the Notes.

## Point 2

**What do the tongues do: swear or acknowledge?**

Here is the most important difference between the two Isaiahs. In the Hebrew of verse 23, every knee "will bow" and every tongue "will swear" — תִּשָּׁבַע, from שָׁבַע, "to swear", the same verb the speaker uses of himself at the start of the verse ("I have sworn"). A tongue that swears to someone is pledging itself to him.

The Greek has a different verb: ἐξομολογήσεται, "will acknowledge / confess / praise," and it adds a recipient the Hebrew does not have: τῷ θεῷ, "to God." Where the Hebrew says "to me every knee will bow" and leaves the tongue's oath without a named object, the Greek spells it out.

What did that Greek verb mean? Our tool lists it in two forms. As ἐξομολογέω it occurs 92 times in the Septuagint and appears in verses where the Hebrew has יָדָה ("give thanks, praise, acknowledge") 80 times — same-verse co-occurrence, not alignment. As ἐξομολογέομαι it occurs 10 times in the New Testament. In the contexts shown, it is used of people openly acknowledging their deeds (Acts 19:18), of confessing wrongs to one another (James 5:16), and of praise (Romans 15:9). In the sample contexts from Josephus it is used of admitting one's own folly and of confessing sins. The common thread in these examples is saying something openly; whether it is praise, admission or allegiance comes from the sentence.

Now look at Philippians 2:11: Paul uses this same Greek verb, ἐξομολογήσηται, not "swear." The wording of Philippians matches the Greek Isaiah, not a word-for-word rendering of the Hebrew. And Paul quotes this verse explicitly in Romans 14:11, with "it is written":

> [GRC Rom.14.11] ὅτι ἐμοὶ κάμψει πᾶν γόνυ, καὶ πᾶσα γλῶσσα ἐξομολογήσεται τῷ θεῷ.
> Literal: that to-me will-bow every knee, and every tongue will-acknowledge to-the God
> Natural: "to me every knee will bow, and every tongue will openly acknowledge God."

So we do not have to guess whether Paul knew this verse as scripture. Romans shows he did.

## Point 3

**Where do the names fall?**

This is where careful reading matters most, so let's go verse by verse and report only what the texts print.

In the Hebrew, the name יְהוָה stands in verses 21, 24 and 25. In the Greek of the same verses, verse 21 has ὁ θεός, verse 24 has no divine name at all, and verse 25 has κυρίου ("from the lord / Lord"). Verse 23 in Hebrew has no divine name; the speaker simply says "to me." The Greek adds τῷ θεῷ, "to God," at the end of that verse.

Now Philippians. Paul places the bowing "at the name of Jesus," and what every tongue acknowledges is [GRC Phil.2.11] κύριος Ἰησοῦς Χριστὸς — "Jesus the Anointed is lord / Lord" — and all of it is [GRC Phil.2.11] εἰς δόξαν θεοῦ πατρός, "to the glory of God the father."

How far does that go? The text gives us more than one honest reading, and you will meet all of them:

- (a) Paul applies to Jesus the homage the speaker of Isaiah 45 claims for himself alone, so κύριος here stands in the place of the God who says "there is no other." Basis: the shared phrase, the "name above every name" in 2:9, and the use of κύριος in the Greek scriptures where the Hebrew has יְהוָה (in the tool's count, the Greek word appears in LXX verses that have the name 5,130 times — same-verse co-occurrence, not alignment).
- (b) The homage still ends where Isaiah's does, with God: Jesus is acknowledged as lord, the exalted master to whom God gave the name, "to the glory of God the father." Basis: κύριος is also an ordinary word for "master, owner" (the tool lists the sense "lord: master", and the Josephus contexts shown use it of people with authority over a land or a decision), and the sentence names θεός as the one glorified.
- (c) Both at once: the sentence keeps the two names distinct and also puts Jesus where Isaiah's speaker stood. Basis: the sentence itself holds κύριος Ἰησοῦς and θεοῦ πατρός together without merging or separating them.

The texts above are the evidence. The grammar does not decide between these, and neither do the capital letters in our English Bibles; that is why you see "lord / Lord" in the translation lines.

## Conclusion

Paul sets the "every knee, every tongue" of Isaiah 45 around the name of Jesus and ends it with the glory of God the father. What that means for how we name Jesus is the question the text leaves us; let the evidence above, not a habit, shape your answer.

For practice this week: read Isaiah 45:18–25 and Philippians 2:5–11 aloud, one after the other. Underline every "I", "me" and name in Isaiah, and every name in Philippians. Then ask of each line: who is speaking, and to whom does the knee bow? Bring your list next week, and let us compare.

"To me every knee will bow, and every tongue will openly acknowledge God."
