# The Shepherd in the Valley — Psalm 23:1–4

## Introduction

Most of us could recite Psalm 23 with our eyes closed. We have heard it at bedsides and at gravesides. That familiarity is a gift, but it carries a risk: we hear the cadence of the old English and stop hearing the claims. Today we will slow down and read four verses in Hebrew, word by word, and learn a few things you can check for yourself in any psalm: what kind of verb you are looking at, who is speaking to whom, and where translations have made choices for you.

The question the psalm answers is simple. When life moves from green fields into a dark valley, what changes, and what does not?

## Text

The psalm is a short song in the book of Psalms; its heading links it to David. In the Hebrew text printed by our tool, verse 1 begins with the divine name; the heading "A psalm of David" appears in the Greek translation (LXX) and in the English versions as part of verse 1. I write the name as YHWH, the four consonants of the text; most English Bibles print "the LORD", following the old reading custom.

> [HEB Ps.23.1] יְהוָה רֹעִי לֹא אֶחְסָר׃
> Literal: YHWH shepherding-me / my-shepherd; not I-lack / I-will-lack
> Natural: YHWH is my shepherd; I lack nothing.

> [HEB Ps.23.2] בִּנְאוֹת דֶּשֶׁא יַרְבִּיצֵנִי עַל־מֵי מְנֻחוֹת יְנַהֲלֵנִי׃
> Literal: in-pastures-of grass he-makes-lie-down-me; by-waters-of rests he-leads-me
> Natural: In grassy pastures he lets me lie down; to waters where I can rest he leads me.

> [HEB Ps.23.3] נַפְשִׁי יְשׁוֹבֵב יַנְחֵנִי בְמַעְגְּלֵי־צֶדֶק לְמַעַן שְׁמוֹ׃
> Literal: my-life/self he-brings-back; he-guides-me in-tracks-of rightness for-the-sake-of his-name
> Natural: He brings me back to life; he guides me along right paths, for the sake of his name.

> [HEB Ps.23.4] גַּם כִּי־אֵלֵךְ בְּגֵיא צַלְמָוֶת לֹא־אִירָא רָע כִּי־אַתָּה עִמָּדִי
> Literal: even when I-walk / I-will-walk in-valley-of deep-darkness / death-shadow, not I-fear harm, for you with-me
> Natural: Even when I walk through a valley of deep darkness, I fear no harm, because you are with me.

> [HEB Ps.23.4] שִׁבְטְךָ וּמִשְׁעַנְתֶּךָ הֵמָּה יְנַחֲמֻנִי׃
> Literal: your-rod and-your-staff, they they-comfort-me
> Natural: your rod and your staff, they comfort me.

## Big Idea

Because YHWH is my shepherd, I lack nothing, in the green pasture and in the dark valley, because he is with me.

Two phrases carry it. At the start, לֹא אֶחְסָר, "I lack nothing". In the middle of the valley, כִּי־אַתָּה עִמָּדִי, "because you are with me". The second phrase explains how the first can still be true in the dark.

## Point 1

**The claim: a shepherd and a lack that is gone (v. 1).**

The psalm opens with two words: the name, and רֹעִי, "my shepherd". The tool tags it as a participle of the verb רָעָה, "to pasture": literally "the one pasturing me". A shepherd in this sense is not a title on a door; it is someone doing a job, every day.

Now the second line, לֹא אֶחְסָר. Here is the first reading skill to take home: **Hebrew verb forms are not tenses.** The tool codes אֶחְסָר as `HVqi1cs`: a yiqtol, first person. Yiqtol can describe the present, a habit, or the future. So "I shall not want" (KJV, BSB), "I do not lack" (YLT) and "I shall lack nothing" (WEB) are all choices the form allows. The Greek translation (LXX) chose the future:

> [LXX Ps.23.1] κύριος ποιμαίνει με καὶ οὐδέν με ὑστερήσει
> Literal: lord shepherds me and nothing me will-lack
> Natural: The Lord shepherds me, and I will lack nothing.

Notice also that the LXX has κύριος where the Hebrew has the divine name YHWH. That is how this Greek translation handled the name; it is useful to know when you meet κύριος in the New Testament.

How we know: run the verse and read the morphology code, not just the English gloss. The gloss says "I lack"; the code tells you the form is open.

## Point 2

**What the shepherd does: four verbs (vv. 2–3).**

Count the verbs in verses 2 and 3: he makes me lie down, he leads me, he brings back, he guides me. In each, the shepherd is the subject and I am the one acted on. The psalm describes a life that is led, not a life that is managed.

The waters are מְנֻחוֹת, from a word the tool glosses "resting". KJV's "still waters" and BSB's "quiet waters" are both trying to say that these are waters where a sheep can rest.

Then comes נַפְשִׁי יְשׁוֹבֵב. Many of us know it as "he restoreth my soul". The noun נֶפֶשׁ is glossed in this verse "self"; it covers life, person, self. The verb is שׁוּב, "turn back, return", here in a form that means "bring back". With נֶפֶשׁ as its object, the phrase can mean bringing someone back to life and strength. "Soul" is not wrong, but if we hear only "my inner spiritual part", we miss how bodily the image is.

He guides me בְמַעְגְּלֵי־צֶדֶק, a construct chain: "tracks of rightness". This can be read as "right paths", paths that go where they should, or as "paths of righteousness", paths marked by right living. The grammar allows both, and in a shepherd's song the two are not far apart. The reason given is לְמַעַן שְׁמוֹ, "for the sake of his name": the shepherd's reputation is on the line in how he leads.

## Point 3

**In the valley: "he" becomes "you" (v. 4).**

Here is the second reading skill: **watch the pronouns.** In verses 1 to 3, the psalmist talks *about* YHWH: "he makes me lie down", "he leads me". In verse 4 he starts talking *to* him: כִּי־אַתָּה עִמָּדִי, "because you are with me". In the dark, the song turns into a prayer.

What is the valley? The word צַלְמָוֶת is the famous one. KJV, BSB and WEB render "the shadow of death"; YLT "death-shade"; the tool glosses it "deep darkness" in this verse and "shadow" in its lexicon. The Greek translation reads it as two words:

> [LXX Ps.23.4] ἐν μέσῳ σκιᾶς θανάτου οὐ φοβηθήσομαι κακά
> Literal: in middle of-shadow of-death not I-will-fear evils
> Natural: in the midst of the shadow of death, I will not fear harm

The Hebrew word occurs 18 times in the tool's data. In Amos it is the darkness that turns into morning:

> [HEB Amos.5.8] וְהֹפֵךְ לַבֹּקֶר צַלְמָוֶת
> Literal: and-turning to-the-morning deep-darkness
> Natural: and he turns deep darkness into morning

So the text allows both: a valley of the deepest darkness, or a valley overshadowed by death. Either way, it is the place where sheep are most afraid, and where the shepherd's rod and staff mean that he is close enough to reach.

## Conclusion

The psalm does not promise that we will never walk through the valley. It says "even when I walk". What it promises is company: "you are with me". And because the shepherd is with us, the first line still holds in the dark: I lack nothing.

Practice for this week: read Psalm 23 once a day. On day one, circle every verb whose subject is YHWH. On day two, mark where "he" becomes "you". Then, in whatever valley you are walking, move from talking *about* God to talking *to* him, in the psalm's own words.

Your rod and your staff, they comfort me.

# A Garrison for the Heart — Philippians 4:4–9

## Opener

Imagine lying awake at three in the morning. Nothing is happening in the room. The house is quiet. But your mind is running laps: the bill, the diagnosis, the conversation you dread, the child who has not called. Your body is in bed; your heart is out in the storm, with no wall around it.

A letter written from a much worse place than our bedrooms speaks to exactly that night.

## The Word

Paul writes to Jesus' followers in Philippi (Philippians 1:1), and he writes in chains (1:13). Just before our passage he urges two women in that community, Euodia and Syntyche, to agree in the Lord (4:2). So the first hearers were a community with tension inside, receiving a letter from a man in custody. Here is what he told them.

> [GRC Phil.4.4] Χαίρετε ἐν κυρίῳ πάντοτε· πάλιν ἐρῶ, χαίρετε.
> Literal: rejoice in [the] lord always; again I-will-say, rejoice
> Natural: Rejoice in the Lord always. I will say it again: rejoice!

> [GRC Phil.4.5] τὸ ἐπιεικὲς ὑμῶν γνωσθήτω πᾶσιν ἀνθρώποις. ὁ κύριος ἐγγύς·
> Literal: the gentleness of-you let-be-known to-all people. the lord near
> Natural: Let your gentleness be known to everyone. The Lord is near.

> [GRC Phil.4.6] μηδὲν μεριμνᾶτε, ἀλλ’ ἐν παντὶ τῇ προσευχῇ καὶ τῇ δεήσει μετ’ εὐχαριστίας τὰ αἰτήματα ὑμῶν γνωριζέσθω πρὸς τὸν θεόν·
> Literal: nothing worry-about, but in everything by-the prayer and by-the asking with thanks the requests of-you let-be-made-known to the God
> Natural: Do not worry about anything; in everything, by prayer and asking, with thanks, let your requests be made known to God.

> [GRC Phil.4.7] καὶ ἡ εἰρήνη τοῦ θεοῦ ἡ ὑπερέχουσα πάντα νοῦν φρουρήσει τὰς καρδίας ὑμῶν καὶ τὰ νοήματα ὑμῶν ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ.
> Literal: and the peace of-the God, the surpassing every mind, will-guard the hearts of-you and the thoughts of-you in Anointed Jesus
> Natural: And God's peace, which goes beyond all understanding, will guard your hearts and your thoughts in the Anointed, Jesus.

> [GRC Phil.4.8] Τὸ λοιπόν, ἀδελφοί, ὅσα ἐστὶν ἀληθῆ, ὅσα σεμνά, ὅσα δίκαια, ὅσα ἁγνά, ὅσα προσφιλῆ, ὅσα εὔφημα, εἴ τις ἀρετὴ καὶ εἴ τις ἔπαινος, ταῦτα λογίζεσθε·
> Literal: the rest, brothers, as-many-as are true, as-many-as honorable, as-many-as right, as-many-as pure, as-many-as lovely, as-many-as well-spoken-of, if any excellence and if any praise, these-things count/consider
> Natural: Finally, brothers and sisters, whatever is true, honorable, right, pure, lovely, well spoken of, if anything is excellent, if anything deserves praise, keep your mind on these things.

> [GRC Phil.4.9] ἃ καὶ ἐμάθετε καὶ παρελάβετε καὶ ἠκούσατε καὶ εἴδετε ἐν ἐμοί, ταῦτα πράσσετε· καὶ ὁ θεὸς τῆς εἰρήνης ἔσται μεθ’ ὑμῶν.
> Literal: what both you-learned and received and heard and saw in me, these-things practice; and the God of-the peace will-be with you
> Natural: What you learned and received, what you heard and saw in me, put it into practice; and the God of peace will be with you.

Three observations for reading this yourself.

First, look at the verb in verse 7: φρουρήσει, "will guard". In the tool's data it is used of a city under guard. Paul uses it of his own escape from Damascus:

> [GRC 2Cor.11.32] ἐφρούρει τὴν πόλιν Δαμασκηνῶν πιάσαι με
> Literal: was-guarding the city of-Damascenes to-seize me
> Natural: he was guarding the city of the Damascenes to arrest me

Josephus uses the same verb for holding a city and its palace. So Paul does not promise a feeling first; he promises a garrison. God's peace stands guard over "hearts and thoughts".

Second, notice what peace guards: τὰ νοήματα, "thoughts". And in verse 8 the command is λογίζεσθε, a verb the tool glosses "to count"; in the LXX it co-occurs most with חָשַׁב "think, reckon" (69 times, same verse, not alignment). Paul is talking about what we keep counting over in our minds.

Third, "the Lord is near", ὁ κύριος ἐγγύς. Two questions sit here, and the text allows more than one answer. Near in *time* (soon to come) or near in *space* (present, at hand)? The tool lists both senses for ἐγγύς and tags this verse with the time sense; KJV and WEB "at hand", YLT and BSB "near". And which lord? The letter calls Jesus κύριος (2:11), and verse 4 has "in the Lord". But the same two words appear in the Greek Psalms:

> [LXX Ps.145.18] ἐγγὺς κύριος πᾶσιν τοῖς ἐπικαλουμένοις αὐτόν
> Literal: near lord to-all those calling-on him
> Natural: The Lord is near to all who call on him.

There the Hebrew has the divine name:

> [HEB Ps.145.18] קָרוֹב יְהוָה לְכָל־קֹרְאָיו
> Literal: near YHWH to-all his-callers
> Natural: YHWH is near to all who call on him.

So the phrase can point to Jesus, whom the letter calls lord, or echo the psalm's wording about YHWH, or both. Paul does not say which; we hold the options open.

## The Anchor Point

Because the Lord is near, you can hand every worry to God in prayer, and his peace will stand guard over your heart and mind.

## Three Ropes

**Rope one: let gentleness show.** The text says, "Let your gentleness be known to everyone", to a community where two women had to be urged to agree (4:2). In life: when you are anxious, you are tempted to be sharp; choose to be the gentle one in the room this week.

**Rope two: turn worry into requests.** The text says, "Do not worry about anything; in everything, by prayer and asking, with thanks, let your requests be made known to God." In life: when the three-in-the-morning list starts, say each item out loud to God, add one thing you are thankful for, and let the guard take the gate.

**Rope three: think it, then do it.** The text says, "keep your mind on these things … put it into practice." In life: pick one true, right and lovely thing each day to count over in your mind, and one thing you have seen faithful people do, and do it.

## Response

You do not have to stand guard over your own heart all night. Paul, in chains, told a community with its own tensions that God's peace would do it. So bring your requests. Bring them tonight, with thanks, to the God who is near.

And the God of peace will be with you.
