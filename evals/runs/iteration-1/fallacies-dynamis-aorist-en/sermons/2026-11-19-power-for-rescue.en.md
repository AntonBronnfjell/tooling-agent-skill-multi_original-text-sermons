# Power Aimed at Rescue — Romans 1:16–17

## Introduction

Most of us have heard it said that the good news is "dynamite". It is a memorable line, and it is meant well. But a memorable line is not the same as what Paul wrote. Today we will let the text set the picture instead of the English dictionary, and we will ask a theological question: what does this passage say God's good news *is*, and what does it do?

## Text

Paul writes to assemblies in Rome he has not yet visited. Before his long argument begins, he states his theme:

> [GRC Rom.1.16] Οὐ γὰρ ἐπαισχύνομαι τὸ εὐαγγέλιον, δύναμις γὰρ θεοῦ ἐστιν εἰς σωτηρίαν
> Literal: not for I-am-ashamed-of the good-news, power for of-God it-is toward rescue/salvation
> Natural: For I am not ashamed of the good news: it is God's power for rescue

> [GRC Rom.1.16] παντὶ τῷ πιστεύοντι, Ἰουδαίῳ τε πρῶτον καὶ Ἕλληνι·
> Literal: to-everyone the [one] believing/trusting, to-Jew both first and to-Greek
> Natural: to everyone who trusts, the Jew first and also the Greek.

> [GRC Rom.1.17] δικαιοσύνη γὰρ θεοῦ ἐν αὐτῷ ἀποκαλύπτεται ἐκ πίστεως εἰς πίστιν
> Literal: righteousness/rightness for of-God in it is-being-revealed from faith/trust to faith/trust
> Natural: For in it God's righteousness is being revealed, from trust to trust,

> [GRC Rom.1.17] καθὼς γέγραπται· Ὁ δὲ δίκαιος ἐκ πίστεως ζήσεται.
> Literal: just-as it-has-been-written: the and righteous from faith/trust will-live
> Natural: as it stands written: "The one who is in the right will live by trust."

Many English Bibles read "the gospel of Christ" in verse 16. Those two extra words are in the later TR and Byzantine texts; the critical editions built on the earliest manuscripts do not have them. The meaning of the verse does not change.

## Big Idea

The good news is God's own power, and it is aimed at rescue — for everyone who trusts.

Three phrases carry it: "God's power", "toward rescue", "to everyone who trusts".

## Point 1

**Not ashamed — of news.**

Paul's word is εὐαγγέλιον, the word our Bibles render "gospel": plainly, *good news*. News is a report of something that has happened. Paul does not say "I am not ashamed of my experience" or "of my program". He is not ashamed of a report.

Why would anyone be ashamed of it? Because it announced a crucified man as God's anointed. Paul says the same elsewhere: the message of the cross is foolishness to some, but to those being rescued it is δύναμις θεοῦ (1Cor.1.18). The shame and the power sit side by side.

## Point 2

**God's power — but what kind?**

You asked me to use the dynamite illustration here. I am not going to, and I want to show you why, because the real picture is better.

Dynamite was invented and named in the 19th century. English borrowed from Greek; Greek never borrowed from English. So "dynamite" cannot tell us anything about what Paul meant. Worse, it points the wrong way: dynamite blows things apart. Paul's power is εἰς σωτηρίαν, toward rescue. It puts people together.

So how was δύναμις actually used? The tool counts it 120 times in the NT and 379 in the Septuagint, the Greek Bible Paul's readers knew. In Septuagint verses it occurs most often alongside the Hebrew words חַיִל (strength, army; 147 verses) and צָבָא (host; 134 verses) — same-verse co-occurrence, not a word-for-word alignment. It is the word for an army, for a king's strength, for capability. Listen to how God speaks before the exodus:

> [LXX Exod.7.4] ἐξάξω σὺν δυνάμει μου τὸν λαόν μου τοὺς υἱοὺς Ισραηλ ἐκ γῆς Αἰγύπτου
> Literal: I-will-lead-out with power/host of-me the people of-me the sons of-Israel out-of land of-Egypt
> Natural: I will lead my people, the sons of Israel, out of the land of Egypt with my power.

There is your illustration. Not an explosion — an exodus. The good news is God's might on the march to bring people out.

## Point 3

**For everyone who trusts — and what the news announces.**

The power reaches "everyone who trusts" (πιστεύοντι, a present participle). It describes the person by what they do — one who trusts. It does not by itself prove "keeps on trusting"; that is a separate question.

Your second illustration was that Jesus said "it is finished" with an aorist, so the aorist means "done once for all". Two problems. First, the word is not an aorist:

> [GRC John.19.30] εἶπεν· Τετέλεσται, καὶ κλίνας τὴν κεφαλὴν παρέδωκεν τὸ πνεῦμα.
> Literal: he-said: it-has-been-finished/completed, and having-bowed the head he-handed-over the spirit/breath
> Natural: he said, "It has been completed," and he bowed his head and gave up his spirit.

The tool tags Τετέλεσται as a perfect passive (V-RPI-3S): an action completed with a state that remains. "It stands finished." That is a real and preachable point. John even uses the same form two verses earlier: Jesus knew that "all things" already τετέλεσται (John.19.28).

Second, the aorist does not mean "once for all" anyway. John 2:20 uses an aorist for a temple that took forty-six years to build. When the NT wants to say "once for all", it uses a word for it:

> [GRC Rom.6.10] τῇ ἁμαρτίᾳ ἀπέθανεν ἐφάπαξ
> Literal: to-the sin he-died once-for-all
> Natural: he died to sin once for all.

So preach both truths from the words that carry them: "it stands completed" from the perfect, "once for all" from ἐφάπαξ.

## Conclusion

What does this passage settle? That Paul's good news is a report about God, not about us; that it is God's own power; that this power is aimed at rescue; and that it reaches everyone who trusts, Jew first and also Greek.

What does it leave open? The exact sense of "God's righteousness" and of "from trust to trust" — the grammar allows more than one reading, and later theological systems have built a great deal on each. We will not settle those today by borrowing someone's system.

The response: don't be ashamed of the news, and don't dress it up with borrowed words. Trust the one it announces. The text itself ends where we end: "The one who is in the right will live by trust."
