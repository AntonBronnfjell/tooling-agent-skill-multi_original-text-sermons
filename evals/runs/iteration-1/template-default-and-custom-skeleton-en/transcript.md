# With Me in the Dark Valley — Psalm 23:1–4

## Introduction

Most of us could recite Psalm 23 half asleep. We hear it at funerals, we see it on hospital walls, we taught it to our children. That familiarity is a gift, but it can also be a blindfold: we stop reading the words and start hearing the tune.

So tonight we will slow down. We will read the first four verses in Hebrew, word by word, and then in plain English. And we will ask the question the psalm itself asks: what does it mean to have a shepherd when the road goes down into the dark? Along the way I want to hand you two or three tools you can use on any psalm.

## Text

The psalm is headed "a psalm of David" in our Bibles, a man who had kept sheep before he kept a kingdom. It is a song, so it moves in short paired lines.

> [HEB Ps.23.1] יְהוָה רֹעִי לֹא אֶחְסָר
> Literal: YHWH [is] shepherd-my; not I-lack
> Natural: YHWH is my shepherd; I lack nothing.

> [HEB Ps.23.2] בִּנְאוֹת דֶּשֶׁא יַרְבִּיצֵנִי עַל־מֵי מְנֻחוֹת יְנַהֲלֵנִי
> Literal: in-pastures-of grass he-makes-lie-down-me; by waters-of rests he-leads-me
> Natural: He lets me lie down in grassy pastures; he leads me to waters where I can rest.

> [HEB Ps.23.3] נַפְשִׁי יְשׁוֹבֵב יַנְחֵנִי בְמַעְגְּלֵי־צֶדֶק לְמַעַן שְׁמוֹ
> Literal: self/life-my he-brings-back; he-guides-me in-tracks-of rightness, for-sake-of name-his
> Natural: He brings me back to life; he guides me along the right tracks, for the sake of his name.

> [HEB Ps.23.4] גַּם כִּי־אֵלֵךְ בְּגֵיא צַלְמָוֶת לֹא־אִירָא רָע
> Literal: even when I-walk in-valley-of deep-darkness/death-shadow, not I-fear harm
> Natural: Even when I walk through a valley of deep darkness, I fear no harm,

> [HEB Ps.23.4] כִּי־אַתָּה עִמָּדִי שִׁבְטְךָ וּמִשְׁעַנְתֶּךָ הֵמָּה יְנַחֲמֻנִי
> Literal: for you [are] with-me; rod-your and-staff-your, they comfort-me
> Natural: because you are with me; your rod and your staff, they comfort me.

The first word is the divine name, four consonants, YHWH. Many English Bibles print "the LORD" in small capitals, following the old Jewish custom of not pronouncing the name. I have kept the consonants so you can see what is on the page.

## Big Idea

The shepherd who feeds and guides his sheep in the open field is the same one who walks beside them in the dark valley, and that presence, not the absence of danger, is why the sheep does not fear.

Two words of the text carry this: "my shepherd" in verse 1 and "with me" in verse 4.

## Point 1

**A shepherd who supplies (vv. 1–2).**

"I lack nothing." The verb אֶחְסָר is a Hebrew form often called the imperfect. Here is a tool you can reuse: Hebrew verb forms are not tenses like English ones. This form can mean "I will lack" or "I do not lack"; the context decides. The King James says "I shall not want"; Young's Literal Translation says "I do not lack". Both are honest. The psalm is describing what life with this shepherd is like, now and going forward.

Then two pictures. The shepherd "makes me lie down" in grassy pastures. Sheep do not lie down when they are hungry or afraid; this is a fed and settled animal. And he leads me to "waters of rests": the noun is plural. These are not rushing streams but places to drink and stop.

How do we know shepherd language is the point? The Greek translation of this psalm, made centuries before Jesus, puts it as a verb:

> [LXX Ps.23.1] κύριος ποιμαίνει με καὶ οὐδέν με ὑστερήσει
> Literal: lord shepherds me, and nothing me will-lack
> Natural: The Lord shepherds me, and I will lack nothing.

In that Greek translation, the verb "to shepherd" appears in verses where the Hebrew has this same shepherding verb 42 times (same-verse co-occurrence, per our tool, not a word-for-word alignment). Shepherding is something this God does, not just a title.

## Point 2

**A shepherd who restores and guides (v. 3).**

"He brings back my life." The noun נֶפֶשׁ is usually printed "soul". Our tool's lexicon says "soul"; its gloss in this verse says "self". In Hebrew this word is the whole living person: breath, appetite, life. The picture is not a ghost inside a body being repaired; it is a worn-out sheep brought back to strength.

Then: "he guides me in tracks of rightness". The word for tracks means worn paths, ruts. And צֶדֶק can be moral "righteousness" or simply "rightness", the correct road. The two may overlap: in a shepherd's mouth, the right path is the one that actually gets you home. Why does he do it? "For the sake of his name." The shepherd's reputation is on the line. A shepherd who loses sheep is a bad shepherd.

## Point 3

**A shepherd who is present in the dark (v. 4).**

"Even when I walk through a valley of" צַלְמָוֶת. Many of us know this as "the shadow of death". Here is an honest choice. Our tool's lexicon glosses the word "shadow"; its in-verse gloss is "deep darkness". The word appears 18 times in the Hebrew Bible. In Amos 5:8 it is the darkness God turns into morning: plain night. In Job 38:17 it stands next to "the gates of death". So the word can mean thick darkness, and it can carry death with it. Both readings are in circulation; the text does not force a narrow one. The valley is a place where you cannot see, and where death may be close.

Now watch the grammar, another tool for your kit. In verses 1 to 3 David talks about the shepherd: "he", "he", "he". In verse 4, in the dark, he starts talking to him: "you are with me". The Hebrew has no verb here at all: literally "you with me". The nearer the danger, the closer the language.

And the rod and staff "comfort me". A rod could beat off an animal; a staff could steady and steer a sheep. What brings comfort is not the absence of wolves but the sight of the shepherd's equipment in the shepherd's hand.

## Conclusion

The shepherd in the meadow and the shepherd in the valley are the same shepherd. The psalm does not promise that the valley will not come. It promises company in it.

This week, try two things. First, pray verse 4 in the second person, out loud: "you are with me". Notice how the psalm turns from talking about God to talking to God. Second, read verses 1 to 4 once a day and circle every "he" and every "you"; ask where you are on that road.

Because you are with me; your rod and your staff, they comfort me.

# A Guarded Mind — Philippians 4:4–9

## Opener

It was three in the morning and my phone screen was the only light in the room. I had opened a note to write down what was keeping me awake, and the list kept growing: the bill, the test results, the conversation I had avoided, the thing my son said at dinner. By the time I put the phone down I had twelve items and no sleep. The list had not solved anything. It had only given my worry a better filing system.

Paul wrote to people who had their own lists, and he gave them a different thing to do with them.

## The Word

Paul writes from custody (Phil 1:13) to the assembly at Philippi, a group he had started and loved. Near the end of the letter he gathers his instructions into a few short lines.

> [GRC Phil.4.4] Χαίρετε ἐν κυρίῳ πάντοτε· πάλιν ἐρῶ, χαίρετε.
> Literal: be-rejoicing in lord always; again I-will-say, be-rejoicing
> Natural: Keep rejoicing in the Lord, always. I will say it again: rejoice!

> [GRC Phil.4.5] τὸ ἐπιεικὲς ὑμῶν γνωσθήτω πᾶσιν ἀνθρώποις. ὁ κύριος ἐγγύς·
> Literal: the gentleness of-you let-be-known to-all people. the lord [is] near
> Natural: Let everyone see how gentle you are. The Lord is near.

> [GRC Phil.4.6] μηδὲν μεριμνᾶτε, ἀλλ’ ἐν παντὶ τῇ προσευχῇ καὶ τῇ δεήσει μετ’ εὐχαριστίας τὰ αἰτήματα ὑμῶν γνωριζέσθω πρὸς τὸν θεόν·
> Literal: nothing be-worrying, but in everything by-the prayer and by-the request with thanks, the asks of-you let-be-made-known toward the God
> Natural: Don't worry about anything; instead, in everything, by prayer and by asking, with thanks, let what you ask for be made known to God.

> [GRC Phil.4.7] καὶ ἡ εἰρήνη τοῦ θεοῦ ἡ ὑπερέχουσα πάντα νοῦν φρουρήσει τὰς καρδίας ὑμῶν καὶ τὰ νοήματα ὑμῶν ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ.
> Literal: and the peace of-the God, the [one] surpassing all mind/understanding, will-guard the hearts of-you and the thoughts of-you in Anointed Jesus
> Natural: And God's peace, which goes beyond anything the mind can grasp, will stand guard over your hearts and your thoughts in the Anointed, Jesus.

> [GRC Phil.4.8] Τὸ λοιπόν, ἀδελφοί, ὅσα ἐστὶν ἀληθῆ, ὅσα σεμνά, ὅσα δίκαια, ὅσα ἁγνά, ὅσα προσφιλῆ, ὅσα εὔφημα, εἴ τις ἀρετὴ καὶ εἴ τις ἔπαινος, ταῦτα λογίζεσθε·
> Literal: the rest, brothers, whatever is true, whatever honorable, whatever right, whatever pure, whatever lovely, whatever well-spoken-of, if any excellence and if any praise, these-things be-counting/reckoning
> Natural: Finally, brothers and sisters: whatever is true, honorable, right, pure, lovely, well spoken of, anything excellent, anything worth praising, keep counting on these things.

> [GRC Phil.4.9] ἃ καὶ ἐμάθετε καὶ παρελάβετε καὶ ἠκούσατε καὶ εἴδετε ἐν ἐμοί, ταῦτα πράσσετε· καὶ ὁ θεὸς τῆς εἰρήνης ἔσται μεθ’ ὑμῶν.
> Literal: what also you-learned and received and heard and saw in me, these-things be-practicing; and the God of-the peace will-be with you
> Natural: What you learned and received from me, what you heard and saw in me, keep doing it. And the God of peace will be with you.

Almost every command here is in the present tense: keep rejoicing, keep not worrying, keep counting, keep doing. In Greek the present views an action as ongoing or habitual; Paul is describing a way of life, not a single moment.

"The Lord is near" can be read two ways. "Near" can be a matter of place: he is close to you now. Or a matter of time: he is coming soon. The text allows both, and either one answers worry.

Then the key promise. The verb φρουρήσει, "will guard", is a soldier's word. In 2 Corinthians 11:32 it is the governor of Damascus guarding the city to catch Paul; the first-century historian Josephus uses it of garrisons guarding a palace, city exits and the fortress of Masada. Philippi was a Roman colony (Acts 16:12); its people knew what a garrison looked like. God's peace is pictured standing at the gate of your heart and your thoughts.

Finally, λογίζεσθε. Our tool glosses it "to count". In the Greek Old Testament it appears in verses where the Hebrew has חָשַׁב, "to reckon, consider", 69 times (same-verse co-occurrence, not alignment). It is more than "think about": it is "take these into account", put them in the ledger you live by.

## The Anchor Point

When worry comes, turn it into a request to God with thanks, and God's peace will stand guard over your heart and mind.

## Three Ropes

**Pray the list.** The text says "in everything … let your requests be made known to God." Take the three-in-the-morning list and turn every line into a sentence that begins with God's name.

**Add thanks.** The text says μετ’ εὐχαριστίας, "with thanks." For every request, name one thing God has already given that is connected to it.

**Change the ledger.** The text says "these things, keep counting." Before you sleep, write down one true, right or lovely thing from the day and let it sit on the page beside your worries.

## Response

You may have come tonight carrying a list. Paul does not tell you to throw it away; he tells you where to send it. Before you leave, take one item, just one, and say it to God, with one word of thanks attached. Then go and practice what you have seen, and see whether the guard shows up at the gate.

And the God of peace will be with you.
