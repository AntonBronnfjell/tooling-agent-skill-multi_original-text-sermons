# What the Text Allows — John 1:1; Galatians 2:16; Genesis 1:26

## Introduction

Some Bible verses have become battlegrounds. People quote them to win arguments, and whole groups define themselves by how they read a single line. If you grew up in a congregation, you probably already "know" what these verses mean. If you didn't, you may suspect that religious people just pick the meaning they like.

Today I want to do something different. We will read three famous verses in the languages they were written in — two in Greek, one in Hebrew — word by word. And at the places where the wording honestly allows more than one reading, I will not tell you which one to take. I will show you the options, the evidence for each, and what the surrounding sentences point to. Then you can weigh it yourself.

## Text

Our three texts come from three very different settings. John 1:1 opens one of the four ancient accounts of Jesus' life. Galatians 2:16 comes from a letter Paul wrote to communities in what is now Turkey, arguing about what puts a person right with God. Genesis 1:26 is from the creation account at the very start of the Hebrew Bible.

## Big Idea

Each of these texts says something clear and also leaves something open — and reading well means hearing both: what the words plainly say, and where their grammar allows more than one meaning.

The clear parts: the word existed in the beginning and was with God; no one is put in the right by works of the law; humankind is made in the image of the one who speaks. The open parts sit in three small grammatical spots: an article missing before θεὸς, a genitive after πίστεως, and a plural "let us".

## Point 1

**John 1:1 — "God", "divine", or "a god"?**

The first two clauses are clear. "In the beginning the word was" — the verb ἦν is imperfect, describing the word as already existing when the beginning began. "And the word was with God" — here God has the article, τὸν θεόν, "the God".

Then the third clause, quoted above: "and θεὸς was the word". Notice what changes. "The word" keeps its article, so it is the subject. But θεὸς now has no article and stands first in the clause. Greek has no word for "a", so an English translator must decide. Three readings are defended:

- (a) **"the word was God"** — definite. Nouns placed before the verb can be definite without the article. Every published version in our comparison (KJV, YLT, BSB, WEB, and the Spanish RV1909 "el Verbo era Dios") takes this reading.
- (b) **"the word was divine" / "what God was, the word was"** — qualitative. The missing article describes the kind of being, not identity with "the God" of the previous clause.
- (c) **"the word was a god"** — indefinite.

A rule often quoted here, Colwell's rule, only observes that definite nouns in this position *usually* lack the article; it does not prove that a noun in this position *is* definite. The immediate context gives you two facts to hold together: the word is "with" God (distinct from "the God"), and θεὸς is said of the word. Verse 2 repeats only the first: "he was in the beginning with God". The grammar does not choose for you.

## Point 2

**Galatians 2:16 — trust *in* the Anointed, or the Anointed's *faithfulness*?**

First, two terms. Χριστός means "anointed": the anointed king Israel expected. Later it became a name, "Christ". And the verb δικαιόω, which many Bibles render "justify", is a courtroom word: to put someone in the right, to declare them in the right. In the Greek Old Testament, it co-occurs most with Hebrew צָדֵק, "be in the right" (21 verses; same-verse co-occurrence, not a word-for-word alignment).

The open question is a little genitive: πίστεως Χριστοῦ, literally "faith of the Anointed" (the first clause has the fuller "of Jesus the Anointed"). Greek genitives mark a relationship, and the reader decides which. Our lookup tool lists πίστις with two senses, "faith" and "faithfulness"; in the Greek Old Testament it co-occurs most with Hebrew אֱמוּנָה, "faithfulness".

- (a) **Objective genitive: "trust in Jesus the Anointed."** BSB and WEB read it so. Support: in the very next clause Paul says "we put our trust *in* the Anointed", using the verb.
- (b) **Subjective genitive: "the faithfulness of Jesus the Anointed."** KJV, YLT and RV1909 ("la fe de Jesucristo") keep "of". Support: the grammar allows it, πίστις can mean faithfulness, and then the middle clause about our trust would not simply repeat the first.

Notice that both readings agree on what the verse denies: no one is put in the right "by works of the law". The debate is about what the alternative rests on — our trust, his faithfulness, or both. The text allows both. The same phrase comes back in Romans 3:22.

## Point 3

**Genesis 1:26 — who is "us"?**

The Hebrew word for God here, אֱלֹהִים, is plural in form. But it takes a singular verb, וַיֹּאמֶר, "and he said". That is how it behaves in Genesis 1:1 too, where "created" is singular. So the plural form alone tells us nothing: in 1 Samuel 28:13 the same word takes a plural participle and means "gods" or divine beings.

What stands out in our verse is not the noun but the speech: נַעֲשֶׂה, "let us make", and "our image", "our likeness". Three readings are defended:

- (a) God addressing a heavenly court or council;
- (b) a plural of majesty or self-deliberation ("let us…" as a way of resolving);
- (c) a plurality within God.

What does the context give us? The very next verse switches to singular: "God created humankind in *his* image". The Greek translation of Genesis keeps the plural:

> [LXX Gen.1.26] καὶ εἶπεν ὁ θεός ποιήσωμεν ἄνθρωπον κατ᾿ εἰκόνα ἡμετέραν καὶ καθ᾿ ὁμοίωσιν
> Literal: and said the God let-us-make human according-to image ours and according-to likeness
> Natural: And God said, "Let us make a human being according to our image and according to likeness,"

The two Dead Sea Scroll fragments in our data that contain this verse preserve only words from its second half; the "us" words are not on the surviving pieces. So the scrolls neither confirm nor deny anything here. The text allows all three readings; the data does not decide.

## Conclusion

Three verses, three small grammatical spots, and in each one the honest answer is: the words allow more than one reading. That is not a weakness of the text. It is an invitation to read slowly, to notice an article, a genitive, a plural, and to hold your conclusions with the weight the evidence actually gives them.

If you are exploring and not sure what you think of the Bible: you don't need to accept anyone's packaged answer. Read these lines yourself, and ask what each word is doing.

If you already follow Jesus: this week, take one verse you are sure about and look for the place where its wording leaves room. Then talk about it with someone who reads it differently — and listen.

"In the beginning the word already was, and the word was with God."
