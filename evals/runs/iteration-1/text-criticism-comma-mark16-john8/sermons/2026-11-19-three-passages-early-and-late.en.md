# Early and Late: Three Famous Passages — 1 John 5:7–8; Mark 16:9–20; John 7:53–8:11

## Introduction

Open a King James Bible and a modern Bible side by side, and in a few places they don't match. One has a sentence the other lacks; one prints a passage in brackets or with a note saying "the earliest manuscripts do not include…". For many believers that is unsettling: which one is the real Bible?

This handout looks at the three best-known cases. For each one we ask the same two questions: what do the editions built mainly on early manuscripts print, and what do the later texts print? One caution up front: the data we use holds printed Greek editions, not individual manuscripts. So we can show what each edition prints and how its editors mark it; we cannot list which ancient manuscript says what.

## Text

Our editions fall into two groups. The critical editions — SBL, NA (Nestle–Aland), WH (Westcott–Hort) and THGNT (Tyndale House) — are built mainly on early manuscripts. The Byzantine text (Byz) represents the majority of later manuscripts, and the Textus Receptus (TR) is the 16th-century printed text behind the King James Version and the Reina-Valera 1909.

**1 John 5:7–8.** Here is the text in SBL, and identically in NA, WH, THGNT and Byz:

> [GRC 1John.5.7] ὅτι τρεῖς εἰσιν οἱ μαρτυροῦντες,
> Literal: because three are the-ones-testifying
> Natural: For there are three who testify:

> [GRC 1John.5.8] τὸ πνεῦμα καὶ τὸ ὕδωρ καὶ τὸ αἷμα, καὶ οἱ τρεῖς εἰς τὸ ἕν εἰσιν.
> Literal: the spirit and the water and the blood, and the three into the one are
> Natural: the spirit, the water and the blood, and the three agree as one.

And here is what the TR alone adds in verse 7:

> [GRC:TR 1John.5.7] ἕν τῷ οὐρανῷ ὁ πατήρ ὁ λόγος καὶ τὸ ἅγιον πνεῦμα καὶ οὗτοι οἱ τρεῖς ἕν εἰσιν
> Literal: in the heaven the father the word and the holy spirit and these the three one are
> Natural: in heaven: the father, the word and the holy spirit, and these three are one.

**Mark 16.** Mark 16:8 ends with the women at the empty tomb:

> [GRC Mark.16.8] καὶ οὐδενὶ οὐδὲν εἶπαν, ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ.
> Literal: and to-no-one nothing they-said, they-were-afraid for
> Natural: and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid.

Then verse 9 begins the longer ending (verses 9–20):

> [GRC Mark.16.9] Ἀναστὰς δὲ πρωῒ πρώτῃ σαββάτου ἐφάνη πρῶτον Μαρίᾳ τῇ Μαγδαληνῇ,
> Literal: having-risen and early on-first of-week he-appeared first to-Mary the Magdalene
> Natural: After he rose early on the first day of the week, he appeared first to Mary Magdalene,

**John 7:53–8:11.** The story of the woman caught in adultery begins and ends like this:

> [GRC John.7.53] Καὶ ἐπορεύθη ἕκαστος εἰς τὸν οἶκον αὐτοῦ,
> Literal: and went each to the house of-him
> Natural: Then each one went home,

> [GRC:NA John.8.11] οὐδὲ ἐγώ σε κατακρίνω· πορεύου καὶ ἀπὸ τοῦ νῦν μηκέτι ἁμάρτανε.
> Literal: neither I you condemn; go and from the now no-longer sin
> Natural: "Neither do I condemn you. Go, and from now on do not sin any more."

## Big Idea

The differences between our Bibles are not hidden: the editions show them openly, and when we look, we find one sentence that only a late printed text has, and two passages that every edition prints but the early-based editions mark as later additions.

The words that carry this idea are the editions' own marks: what is printed, what is missing, and the double brackets [[ ]].

## Point 1

**1 John 5:7–8: a sentence only the TR has.**

This is the clearest case. The words "in heaven, the father, the word and the holy spirit, and these three are one" and, in verse 8, "and there are three who testify on earth" appear in the TR only. Every added word is tagged TR and labelled "late" in our variant data. SBL, NA, WH and THGNT do not have them — and neither does the Byzantine text, the text of the majority of later manuscripts. So the addition is not just "missing from the early manuscripts"; in our data it is missing from every edition except the 16th-century TR.

That is why the King James Version and the Reina-Valera 1909 have it, and the BSB and WEB do not. The older English YLT prints it in square brackets.

What remains in all editions is still a striking sentence: three witnesses — the spirit, the water, the blood — agreeing as one. Removing the late words does not remove the passage's point.

## Point 2

**Mark 16:9–20: printed everywhere, bracketed by the editors.**

Here the situation is different. All editions in our data print verses 9–20, the TR and Byz included. But NA and WH enclose them in double brackets [[ ]], which is how those editors mark text they judge to be a later addition. In our data, the bracket also shows up at the start of the passage in THGNT, TR and Byz; that marker is inherited in the data and should not be read as those editions' own judgment. The SBL text prints the verses without brackets. The only wording variant the tool lists in verse 9 is small: ἀφ᾽ (TR, Byz) or παρ᾽ (the critical editions), both meaning "from" whom he had cast out seven demons.

There is a second surprise. After 16:8, SBL, NA and WH also print a short ending — two sentences beginning "They briefly reported everything they had been told to Peter and those with him" — and NA and WH bracket that too. The TR and Byz do not have it. So the early-based editions show that Mark's ending circulated in more than one form.

## Point 3

**John 7:53–8:11: a well-loved story, bracketed in the critical editions.**

This passage is printed in all editions in our data. NA and WH open double brackets at 7:53 and close them at the end of 8:11, again marking a passage the editors judge to be later. The TR and Byz print it too, with the same inherited marker in our data.

The editions also differ in wording inside the story. In 8:11, NA, WH and TR read κατακρίνω, "condemn"; Byz reads κρίνω, "judge". NA and WH have "from now on"; TR and Byz do not. And our THGNT data has verses 8:1–10 but no separate entry for 8:11 — we cannot tell from the data whether that is a numbering difference.

Here is a "how we know" exercise for your group: read John 7:52 and then jump to 8:12. Then read the story in between. Notice what the brackets ask us to consider, and notice that the editors still print the story.

## Conclusion

Three passages, three different situations: a sentence found only in the TR; an ending printed everywhere but bracketed by the early-based editors; a story printed everywhere and bracketed too. None of this is a secret, and none of it changes what the rest of the text says. It does ask us to be honest about which words are early and which are late.

Practice for this week: take your own Bible to these three places. Look for a footnote, brackets or a missing verse number. Then ask: what does my Bible's note say, and does it match what the editions show?

"Neither do I condemn you. Go, and from now on do not sin any more."
