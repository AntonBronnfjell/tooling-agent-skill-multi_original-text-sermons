# Built, Filled, Immersed — Matthew 16:18; Acts 2:4; Acts 2:38

## Introduction

Some words are so familiar that we stop hearing them. *Church. Holy Ghost. Baptize. Repent.* We say them every week, and each one comes with centuries of buildings, rituals and arguments attached. That is not bad in itself, but it means that when we open the Bible, we often hear our own vocabulary instead of the words the first listeners heard.

In this study we will take three short texts — one promise of Jesus and two moments from the day of Pentecost — and read them in the Greek in which they were written. For each one we will make a word-for-word translation, then a natural one. And we will ask a simple question you can take to any passage: what did this word mean to the people who first used it?

## Text

The first text is Jesus' answer to Peter near Caesarea Philippi, right after Peter says, "You are the Anointed, the son of the living God" (Matthew 16:16).

> [GRC Matt.16.18] κἀγὼ δέ σοι λέγω ὅτι σὺ εἶ Πέτρος, καὶ ἐπὶ ταύτῃ τῇ πέτρᾳ οἰκοδομήσω μου τὴν ἐκκλησίαν,
> Literal: and-I also to-you say that you are Petros/Rock, and upon this the rock I-will-build my the assembly
> Natural: And I tell you: you are Peter, "Rock," and on this rock I will build my assembly,

> [GRC Matt.16.18] καὶ πύλαι ᾅδου οὐ κατισχύσουσιν αὐτῆς·
> Literal: and gates of-Hades not will-overpower it
> Natural: and the gates of Hades will not overpower it.

The second and third come from Acts 2, the feast of Pentecost in Jerusalem, a few weeks after the resurrection.

> [GRC Acts.2.4] καὶ ἐπλήσθησαν πάντες πνεύματος ἁγίου,
> Literal: and were-filled all with-holy spirit
> Natural: And all of them were filled with the holy spirit,

> [GRC Acts.2.4] καὶ ἤρξαντο λαλεῖν ἑτέραις γλώσσαις καθὼς τὸ πνεῦμα ἐδίδου ἀποφθέγγεσθαι αὐτοῖς.
> Literal: and they-began to-speak in-other tongues/languages as the spirit was-giving to-utter/declare to-them
> Natural: and they began to speak in other languages, as the spirit was giving them to speak out.

> [GRC Acts.2.38] Πέτρος δὲ πρὸς αὐτούς· Μετανοήσατε, καὶ βαπτισθήτω ἕκαστος ὑμῶν ἐπὶ τῷ ὀνόματι Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ
> Literal: Peter and to them: repent/turn (all-of-you), and let-be-immersed each of-you upon the name of-Jesus Anointed
> Natural: Peter said to them, "Repent, all of you, and let each one of you be immersed on the name of Jesus the Anointed

> [GRC Acts.2.38] εἰς ἄφεσιν τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ὑμῶν, καὶ λήμψεσθε τὴν δωρεὰν τοῦ ἁγίου πνεύματος·
> Literal: into/toward release of-the sins of-you, and you-will-receive the gift of-the holy spirit
> Natural: toward the release of your sins, and you will receive the gift of the holy spirit."

## Big Idea

Jesus promised to build an assembly of people that death itself cannot overpower, and at Pentecost he began: people filled with God's spirit, speaking so others understood, and inviting everyone to turn and be immersed on his name.

The words that carry this idea are plain words: "assembly", "filled", "turn", "be immersed", "release", "gift". Under each of them sits a familiar church word.

## Point 1

**An assembly, not a building — and a rock with options.**

The word our Bibles render *church* is ἐκκλησίαν: an assembly, a gathering of people. How do we know? Run the word through the data. It appears 77 times in the Greek Old Testament, and in 66 of those verses the Hebrew has קָהָל, the assembly of Israel (that is same-verse co-occurrence, not a word-for-word alignment). The Jewish historian Josephus uses it for Moses gathering the people. Luke uses it in Acts 19:32 for a confused civic crowd in Ephesus. So when Jesus says "my assembly", his first listeners would hear "my gathered people" — not a building, and not yet a denomination.

Watch out for a popular move: "ἐκκλησία comes from 'out' + 'call', so it means 'the called-out ones'." That is a root fallacy. A word means what its users meant by it, and the usage shows "assembly", not "called out".

Now the rock. Peter's name is Πέτρος (masculine); the word for "rock" in the next clause is πέτρᾳ (feminine), with a near demonstrative, "this". Readers have taken "this rock" in three ways: (a) Peter himself, because of the obvious wordplay on his name; (b) Peter's confession in verse 16, "you are the Anointed"; (c) Jesus himself. The grammar does not settle it: the wordplay favors (a), the change of gender is used to argue for (b) or (c). As a teacher, give your group all three and let the text speak.

One more word: ᾅδου, "of Hades", the world of the dead. The King James and Reina-Valera 1909 say "hell" here. But Hades and Gehenna are different words in the New Testament, and merging them changes the picture: Jesus promises that the gates of death will not hold his people.

## Point 2

**Filled with the holy spirit — in languages people understood.**

In Acts 2:4 the verb ἐπλήσθησαν is passive: "they were filled". The disciples did not fill themselves. And notice the capitalization question: the manuscripts of this period have no capital letters, so "Holy Spirit" with capitals, or the King James "Holy Ghost", is a translation decision. I keep "holy spirit" in lower case on the translation lines so we read what the text shows: God's spirit doing something.

What did it do? The text says they spoke ἑτέραις γλώσσαις, "in other tongues". γλῶσσα means the physical tongue, and also a language; in the Greek Old Testament it co-occurs with Hebrew לָשׁוֹן, which has the same range. Here the context decides. Two verses later the crowd says each one heard them "in his own dialect" (Acts 2:6, 2:8), and in 2:11 the visitors say, "we hear them speaking in our own languages the great deeds of God". So in this passage the "tongues" are languages the hearers recognized. That is a reusable habit: let the next few verses define the word.

## Point 3

**Turn, be immersed, receive: Peter's answer.**

The crowd asks what to do, and Peter answers with two verbs. First Μετανοήσατε, "repent" — a standard rendering, but let the usage fill it in. In the Greek Old Testament this verb co-occurs most with Hebrew נָחַם (14 times) and שׁוּב (9 times): to change one's mind, to turn back. Then βαπτισθήτω, which our Bibles render *be baptized*. The word itself means to dip, immerse or wash: the Greek Old Testament uses it of Naaman dipping himself in the Jordan (2 Kings 5:14), and Josephus uses it of ships sinking. I render it "be immersed".

Here is a teaching observation anyone can reuse: check the person and number. "Repent" is second person plural, "all of you". "Let … be immersed" is third person singular, "each one". Then comes εἰς ἄφεσιν, "toward release" of your sins. ἄφεσις is a release word; in the Greek Old Testament it often stands in verses about the year of jubilee, when debts and slaves were released.

This verse has real options, and a Christian audience will usually hold one of them already:

- **What εἰς means here:** (a) purpose, "for the release of sins"; (b) "with a view to / in connection with" the release.
- **What it attaches to:** (a) both verbs lead toward the release; (b) the shift in number ties "release of your sins" (plural "your") to the plural "repent".
- **"On the name":** SBL, NA, THGNT, TR and Byz read ἐπὶ, "on/upon the name"; WH and Tregelles read ἐν, "in the name". And Acts phrases the name differently elsewhere (Acts 8:16 and 19:5 "into the name of the lord Jesus"; Acts 10:48 "in the name of Jesus the Anointed"). Readers take these as (a) a spoken formula, (b) an idiom meaning "belonging to / by the authority of" Jesus, or (c) both.

The text allows these readings. Show them, and let the group see the evidence.

## Conclusion

Three texts, one movement: Jesus promised to build his assembly; the spirit filled it; Peter invited people into it. Under our familiar words are plain ones — assembly, filled, turn, immerse, release, gift — and those plain words are often sharper than the familiar ones.

Practice for this week: take one familiar church word (church, baptize, repent, saint) and look it up in the passage where it first appears in your reading. Ask: what is the plain word underneath? What do the next few verses show it means? Then turn back to God where you need to, and remember that you belong to an assembly the gates of Hades will not overpower.
