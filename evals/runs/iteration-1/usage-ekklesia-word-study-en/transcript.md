# ἐκκλησία: what the word meant before and around the New Testament

*A word study for a Bible class. Every Greek and Hebrew word below comes from the local database (`lookup.py`), and the counts are the tool's counts.*

## 1. The basic sense: a gathering of people

The tool's lexicon glosses ἐκκλησία as **"assembly"**. It counts **117** uses in the New Testament, **77** in the Septuagint (LXX, the Greek Old Testament) and **71** in Josephus and Philo combined. The tool doesn't split that last figure between the two authors, and every sample context it returned for that group is from Josephus. The word means a body of people who have gathered. What kind of gathering it is comes from the context. Four kinds of evidence show that range.

## 2. In the Greek Old Testament: Israel gathered before God

In LXX verses, ἐκκλησία co-occurs with the Hebrew word קָהָל ("assembly, congregation") **66 times**. That is a same-verse count, not a word-for-word alignment. The best-known setting is the day at Horeb (Sinai):

> [LXX Deut.4.10] ἐν Χωρηβ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῆς ἐκκλησίας ὅτε εἶπεν κύριος πρός με ἐκκλησίασον πρός με τὸν λαόν
> Literal: in Horeb on-the day of-the assembly, when said lord to me: assemble to me the people
> Natural: at Horeb, on the day of the assembly, when the Lord said to me, "Gather the people to me."

The Hebrew of the same verse has the verb הַקְהֶל־, glossed "assemble". The Greek uses the noun and the matching verb together: on "the day of the assembly", the people are assembled. So the word names a group **called together to hear**.

The word isn't reserved for God's people, though. A psalm uses it for a gathering the writer hates:

> [LXX Ps.26.5] ἐμίσησα ἐκκλησίαν πονηρευομένων
> Literal: I-hated assembly of-evildoers
> Natural: I hate the gathering of evildoers.

The Hebrew of that verse is קְהַל מְרֵעִים. In 1 Samuel 19:20 (LXX) the word is used for a band of prophets: τὴν ἐκκλησίαν τῶν προφητῶν. On its own, the word says *a gathering*. Who gathered, and why, comes from the words around it.

## 3. In Josephus (1st century AD): the people called together

Josephus wrote in Greek in the same generation as most of the New Testament. Retelling the Sinai story, he writes:

> [EXT:Josephus Ant.3.84] συγκαλεῖ τὸ πλῆθος εἰς ἐκκλησίαν ἀκουσόμενον ὧν ὁ θεὸς εἴποι πρὸς αὐτόν
> Literal: he-calls-together the multitude into assembly, about-to-hear what the God might-say to him
> Natural: he called the people together into an assembly to hear what God had said to him.

Later, after the report of the spies, the gathering breaks up:

> [EXT:Josephus Ant.3.306] διαλυθέντες ἐκ τῆς ἐκκλησίας σὺν γυναιξὶ καὶ παισὶν ὀλοφυρόμενοι διῆγον
> Literal: having-been-dispersed from the assembly, with wives and children lamenting they-passed-[the-time]
> Natural: when the assembly broke up, they spent their time lamenting, with their wives and children.

In both passages the ἐκκλησία is an event: people are called *into* it and go home *from* it. The tool flags no editor-bracketed text in either passage.

## 4. Inside the New Testament: the same word for very different gatherings

**A civic crowd in Ephesus.** Luke uses the word for the riot in the theatre:

> [GRC Acts.19.32] ἦν γὰρ ἡ ἐκκλησία συγκεχυμένη, καὶ οἱ πλείους οὐκ ᾔδεισαν τίνος ἕνεκα συνεληλύθεισαν.
> Literal: was for the assembly confused, and the most not knew for-what-reason they-had-come-together
> Natural: The assembly was in confusion, and most of them didn't know why they had come together.

The town clerk then points them to the proper one, ἐν τῇ ἐννόμῳ ἐκκλησίᾳ, "in the lawful assembly" (Acts 19:39), and at the end ἀπέλυσεν τὴν ἐκκλησίαν, "he dismissed the assembly" (Acts 19:41). So the word still had its ordinary civic meaning in the New Testament world.

**Israel in the wilderness.** In Stephen's speech the word looks back to Sinai, as the LXX did:

> [GRC Acts.7.38] οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ γενόμενος ἐν τῇ ἐκκλησίᾳ ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ
> Literal: this is the [one] having-been in the assembly in the wilderness
> Natural: This is the man who was in the assembly in the wilderness.

**The followers of Jesus.** Acts 8:1 speaks of τὴν ἐκκλησίαν τὴν ἐν Ἱεροσολύμοις, "the assembly in Jerusalem". In Acts 8:3 Saul attacks it κατὰ τοὺς οἴκους, "house by house". Those who first used the word this way already had both meanings in their ears: Israel gathered before God, and a town's citizens called to meet.

## 5. What your English Bible does with it

The Greek word is the same in each of these verses. The English changes:

| verse | KJV | BSB | others |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acts 19:32 (Ephesus crowd) | "assembly" | "assembly" | RV1909 "concurrencia" |
| Acts 7:38 (Israel at Sinai) | "**church** in the wilderness" | "assembly" | YLT "assembly" |
| Matt 16:18 (Jesus' own) | "my **church**" | "My **church**" | YLT, WEB "my assembly"; RV1909 "mi iglesia" |

Translators who use "church" for Jesus' followers and "assembly" for the Ephesus crowd are deciding by context. The Greek makes no such distinction. That's a fair choice, but it is the translator's choice. "Church" also brings later associations, such as buildings, denominations and offices, that 1st-century hearers of ἐκκλησία didn't have. It's a useful exercise to read a New Testament passage with "assembly" or "gathering" in place of "church" and see what changes. (Matthew 16:18 also has a well-known contested question about which "rock" is meant. The options are Peter, Peter's confession, or Jesus. That question is separate from the meaning of the word.)

## 6. A move to avoid: "the called-out ones"

You'll often hear that ἐκκλησία = ἐκ ("out") + καλέω ("call"), so it means "the called-out ones", a people separated from the world. That is the **root fallacy**: building meaning from a word's parts instead of from how people used it. None of the evidence above uses the word that way. The Ephesus crowd was not "called out of the world", the evildoers in Psalm 26 were not either, and Josephus' Israelites were called *into* an assembly. Whatever the New Testament teaches about God's people being set apart, it teaches with other words and sentences, not with the etymology of this one.

## 7. Two caveats

- **Papyri.** The tool also returns a Moulton–Milligan lexicon entry. It is noisy OCR of a printed book, so I only paraphrase it and don't quote it as ancient text. The entry reports a bilingual inscription from the theatre at Ephesus, which it dates to AD 103–4, mentioning statues to be set up for the ἐκκλησία there. It also reports an assembly at Apamea that the lexicon describes as made up of the whole population. Both fit the civic sense of Acts 19.
- **Limits of the data.** The tool returns only five sample contexts from Josephus and Philo, and it can't list all 71 occurrences or separate Josephus from Philo. I haven't checked Philo's usage, so I make no claim about it.

**In one sentence:** in the first century, ἐκκλησία meant an assembly, a gathering of people called together. The Greek Old Testament used it for Israel gathered before God, Greek cities used it for their citizens' meeting, and Jesus' followers used it for their own gatherings. The word itself never meant "the called-out ones".
