# What the Text Says, and Where It Leaves Room — John 1:1; Galatians 2:16; Genesis 1:26

## Introduction

Some sentences have been argued over for so long that people know the argument better than the sentence. Mention "the Word was God", "faith in Christ" or "let us make man in our image", and many of us can already hear the debate: whose side are you on?

Tonight we are going to do something less exciting and more useful. We will read three famous sentences slowly, in the languages they were written in, and ask only two questions: what does the text clearly say, and where does its grammar leave more than one door open? I will not tell you which door to walk through. Whether you come to church every week or you are just curious, you will leave able to check these sentences yourself.

## Text

Three texts, one for each point: John 1:1 (Point 1), Galatians 2:16 (Point 2), Genesis 1:26 (Point 3).

John 1:1 opens a first-century account of Jesus' life, written in Greek. Its first sentence:

> [GRC John.1.1] Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν,
> Literal: in beginning was the word, and the word was with/toward the God
> Natural: In the beginning was the word, and the word was with God,

> [GRC John.1.1] καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.
> Literal: and God/divine/a-god was the word
> Natural: (options) and the word was God / and what God was, the word was / and the word was a god.

Galatians is a first-century letter from Paul, a Jewish teacher, to groups of Jesus-followers in Galatia, in what is now Turkey:

> [GRC Gal.2.16] εἰδότες δὲ ὅτι οὐ δικαιοῦται ἄνθρωπος ἐξ ἔργων νόμου ἐὰν μὴ διὰ πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ,
> Literal: knowing and that not is-made/declared-right a-person from works of-law except through faith/faithfulness of-Jesus Anointed
> Natural: We know that a person is not put right by works of the law, but through (options) trust in Jesus the Anointed / the faithfulness of Jesus the Anointed.

> [GRC Gal.2.16] καὶ ἡμεῖς εἰς Χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν ἐπιστεύσαμεν, ἵνα δικαιωθῶμεν ἐκ πίστεως Χριστοῦ καὶ οὐκ ἐξ ἔργων νόμου,
> Literal: also we into Anointed Jesus trusted, so-that we-may-be-made/declared-right from faith/faithfulness of-Anointed and not from works of-law
> Natural: So we too put our trust in the Anointed, Jesus, so that we might be put right (options) by trust in the Anointed / by the Anointed's faithfulness, and not by works of the law.

Genesis 1 is the opening of the Hebrew scriptures, the creation account:

> [HEB Gen.1.26] וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים נַעֲשֶׂה אָדָם בְּצַלְמֵנוּ כִּדְמוּתֵנוּ
> Literal: and-said God let-us-make humankind in-image-of-us according-to-likeness-of-us
> Natural: Then God said, "Let us make humankind in our image, according to our likeness."

None of the printed editions in our data disagree on the wording of John 1:1; in Galatians 2:16 their differences do not touch the phrases we discuss (see Notes).

## Big Idea

In each of these sentences God acts first — a word that was with God in the beginning, a rightness that comes not from works of the law but through the πίστις of Jesus, a God who says "let us make humankind in our image" — and in each, one phrase is left open by the grammar itself.

## Point 1

**John 1:1 — "and the word was …"**

What is clear? Three clauses, the same verb ἦν ("was") three times. The word already "was" in the beginning; the word was "with" God (πρὸς τὸν θεόν); and the last clause says something about what the word was.

Here is a tool for reading any Greek sentence: look for the article, the little word "the". In the second clause, God has the article: τὸν θεόν, "the God". In the third, θεὸς has no article, and it comes first, before the verb. The subject is ὁ λόγος, "the word", the one with the article.

What does a noun with no article, placed first, mean? Grammarians give three options:

- (a) "the word was God": definite. Predicate nouns placed before the verb can be definite without the article. A rule called Colwell's rule observes that definite predicates before the verb usually lack the article, but it does not prove that this one is definite.
- (b) "what God was, the word was" / "the word was divine": qualitative, describing the kind of thing the word was.
- (c) "the word was a god": indefinite.

Every translation in our comparison (KJV, BSB, WEB, YLT, and the Spanish RV1909) prints option (a), with a capital "Word". Those are translators' decisions; the Greek of this period had no capital letters. The immediate context gives you two facts to weigh: the second clause distinguishes the word from "the God" it was with, and the third clause puts θεὸς in front. The text allows each reading to be argued. That is the finding.

## Point 2

**Galatians 2:16 — "the faith of Jesus" or "faith in Jesus"?**

What is clear? Paul says three times that a person is not put right "by works of law" (ἐξ ἔργων νόμου). The verb δικαιόω means "to make or declare right, to put in the right". Many Bibles say "justified". In the Greek Old Testament this verb appears in verses where the Hebrew has צָדֵק ("be in the right") 21 times and שָׁפַט ("judge") 9 times (same-verse co-occurrence, not word-for-word alignment), which is courtroom vocabulary. Here the verb is passive: someone else does the putting right.

What is also clear: "we put our trust in" the Anointed: εἰς Χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν ἐπιστεύσαμεν. ("Christ" is not a surname; Χριστός means "anointed", the king Israel expected.)

What is open? The phrase πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, literally "faith of Jesus the Anointed". πίστις can mean "faith, trust" and also "faithfulness": our tool lists both senses. And a genitive ("of") can point either way:

- (a) objective genitive: "trust in Jesus the Anointed" — Jesus is the one trusted. BSB and WEB choose this.
- (b) subjective genitive: "the faithfulness of Jesus the Anointed" — Jesus is the one who is faithful. KJV, YLT and RV1909 say "the faith of".

The same construction (πίστις + a genitive) appears elsewhere, and translations read it both ways. In Romans 3:3 Paul asks whether people's unfaithfulness will cancel [GRC Rom.3.3] τὴν πίστιν τοῦ θεοῦ, "the faithfulness of God" — God's own (BSB, WEB, YLT). In Mark 11:22 Jesus says [GRC Mark.11.22] Ἔχετε πίστιν θεοῦ, which BSB, KJV and WEB render "have faith in God" and YLT "have faith of God". Grammar allows both in Galatians 2:16. Notice that the sentence already says "we trusted into the Anointed"; readers of (a) see Paul repeating it, readers of (b) see Paul pairing Jesus' faithfulness with our trust. The text allows both.

## Point 3

**Genesis 1:26 — "let us make … in our image"**

What is clear? The subject is אֱלֹהִים, a noun plural in form. But the verb before it, וַיֹּאמֶר ("and he said"), is singular, as is בָּרָא ("he created") in Genesis 1:1. When the same noun takes a plural verb, it means "gods": in 1 Samuel 28:13 a woman says she sees אֱלֹהִים "coming up", with a plural participle. So in Genesis 1:26 the form "God" is not what is contested.

What is open is the speech itself: נַעֲשֶׂה, "let us make", and the "our" on "image" and "likeness". The next verse returns to the singular:

> [HEB Gen.1.27] וַיִּבְרָא אֱלֹהִים׀ אֶת־הָאָדָם בְּצַלְמוֹ
> Literal: and-created God the-humankind in-image-of-him
> Natural: So God created humankind in his image.

The old Greek translation keeps the plural, "let us make", "our image":

> [LXX Gen.1.26] καὶ εἶπεν ὁ θεός ποιήσωμεν ἄνθρωπον κατ᾿ εἰκόνα ἡμετέραν καὶ καθ᾿ ὁμοίωσιν
> Literal: and said the God, let-us-make human according-to image ours and according-to likeness
> Natural: And God said, "Let us make a human being according to our image and according to likeness."

And the Dead Sea Scrolls? Two scrolls in our data have pieces of this verse, but the surviving letters do not include the "let us / our" words. We have no scroll witness for them.

Three readings are offered: (a) God addresses a heavenly court or council; (b) a plural of majesty or of deliberation, God speaking to himself; (c) a plurality within God. The verse itself does not choose. What it does say plainly: humankind is made to bear God's image, and to rule over the fish, the birds and the land animals.

## Conclusion

Three famous sentences, each clear about God acting first, and each with a phrase the grammar leaves open. That is not a weakness of the Bible; it is an invitation to read carefully.

If you are exploring and don't share the faith: you have just read these words as they stand, without anyone telling you what to think. Keep going. Read John chapter 1 this week and ask who this "word" is.

If you already follow Jesus: practice holding your reading with an open hand. When you meet someone who takes the other option, show them the Greek or the Hebrew, not the label. And remember the clear part of each sentence: "In the beginning was the word, and the word was with God."
