# Power for Rescue, a Work Finished — Romans 1:16; John 19:30; Romans 6:10

## Introduction

Two phrases sit near the center of how many of us talk about the good news. "The power of God." "It is finished." We have heard them illustrated many times, sometimes with dynamite and sometimes with grammar charts.

Tonight we let the words themselves do the work. What did δύναμις mean to people who used it in the first century? What form of the verb did Jesus actually use on the cross? And where does the New Testament say "once for all"? The honest answers turn out to preach better than the illustrations.

## Text

Three texts, one for each point: Romans 1:16 (Point 1), John 19:30 (Point 2), Romans 6:10 (Point 3).

> [GRC Rom.1.16] Οὐ γὰρ ἐπαισχύνομαι τὸ εὐαγγέλιον, δύναμις γὰρ θεοῦ ἐστιν εἰς σωτηρίαν παντὶ τῷ πιστεύοντι, Ἰουδαίῳ τε πρῶτον καὶ Ἕλληνι
> Literal: not for I-am-ashamed-of the good-news; power for of-God it-is toward rescue/salvation to-everyone the-one trusting/believing, to-Jew both first and to-Greek
> Natural: For I am not ashamed of the good news: it is God's power for rescue to everyone who trusts, to the Jew first and also to the Greek.

> [GRC John.19.30] ὅτε οὖν ἔλαβεν τὸ ὄξος ὁ Ἰησοῦς εἶπεν· Τετέλεσται, καὶ κλίνας τὴν κεφαλὴν παρέδωκεν τὸ πνεῦμα.
> Literal: when then took the sour-wine the Jesus, he-said: it-has-been-finished/accomplished; and having-bowed the head he-handed-over the spirit/breath
> Natural: When Jesus had taken the sour wine, he said, "It is finished." Then he bowed his head and handed over the spirit.

> [GRC Rom.6.10] ὃ γὰρ ἀπέθανεν, τῇ ἁμαρτίᾳ ἀπέθανεν ἐφάπαξ
> Literal: what for he-died, to-the sin he-died once/once-for-all
> Natural: For the death he died, he died to sin once for all.

Some printed editions (TR, Byz) read "the good news **of the Anointed**" in Romans 1:16. NA, SBL, WH and THGNT don't have those words. That's why KJV says "the gospel of Christ" and BSB says only "the gospel".

## Big Idea

The good news is God's power aimed at rescue. Jesus declared his work completed, and Paul says his death to sin happened once for all, with a word made for that job.

Three words carry it: δύναμις ("power", aimed εἰς σωτηρίαν, "toward rescue"), Τετέλεσται ("it has been finished"), and ἐφάπαξ ("once for all").

## Point 1

**Power aimed at rescue, Romans 1:16. Not dynamite.**

*About the dynamite illustration:* I'd leave it out. The English word "dynamite" comes from the Greek (background, not from our data: it was coined in modern times). The influence runs from Greek to English and never back, so Paul's readers could not hear "explosive" in δύναμις. Reading a later meaning back into an ancient word is the anachronism fallacy.

What did the word mean? `word δύναμις` gives NT 119 (SBL) and LXX 379. In LXX verses it appears together with Hebrew חַ֫יִל (strength, army) 147 times and צָבָא (host) 134 times. That is same-verse co-occurrence, not alignment. In the contexts `usage δύναμις` shows, it is the force of an army (LXX Gen.21.22: Phikol, the commander of "his δύναμις", his army). It is also God's might bringing his people out:

> [LXX Exod.7.4] ἐξάξω σὺν δυνάμει μου τὸν λαόν μου τοὺς υἱοὺς Ισραηλ ἐκ γῆς Αἰγύπτου
> Literal: I-will-lead-out with power/host of-me the people of-me, the sons of-Israel, from land of-Egypt
> Natural: I will bring my people, the children of Israel, out of the land of Egypt with my power.

*A better illustration, from usage:* the Exodus. God's δύναμις is not a blast that scatters things. It is strength that leads people out. Romans 1:16 gives the power a direction: εἰς σωτηρίαν, "toward rescue", for "everyone who trusts".

- Text says: the good news *is* God's power, for rescue, to everyone who trusts.
- Within Paul's argument: Jew first and also Greek. The same power is for both.
- Later systems: theologies of conversion and grace have been built on this verse. Here we stay with what it says.

## Point 2

**"It has been finished", John 19:30. A perfect, not an aorist.**

*About the aorist illustration:* the premise doesn't hold. The tool tags Τετέλεσται as `V-RPI-3S`, a **perfect** passive indicative, not an aorist. And the claim behind it doesn't hold either: the aorist does not mean "once for all, never repeated". It presents an action as a whole. A forty-six-year building project is told in the aorist:

> [GRC John.2.20] Τεσσεράκοντα καὶ ἓξ ἔτεσιν οἰκοδομήθη ὁ ναὸς οὗτος
> Literal: forty and six years was-built the temple this
> Natural: This temple took forty-six years to build.

(οἰκοδομήθη, `V-API-3S`, aorist.) Preaching "aorist = once for all" is overspecification: it claims more precision than the form carries.

What the perfect does show: a completed action with a resulting state. "It has been finished", and it stands finished. That's the form. It doesn't tell us by itself everything that was finished. For that, John gives us context two verses earlier:

> [GRC John.19.28] εἰδὼς ὁ Ἰησοῦς ὅτι ἤδη πάντα τετέλεσται ἵνα τελειωθῇ ἡ γραφὴ
> Literal: knowing the Jesus that already all-things have-been-finished, so-that may-be-completed the writing
> Natural: Jesus, knowing that everything had now been finished, so that the scripture would be completed…

*A better illustration, from usage:* in LXX Ruth 3:18 a woman tells her "daughter" to sit and wait, because the man will not rest until he finishes the matter today:

> [LXX Ruth.3.18] οὐ γὰρ μὴ ἡσυχάσῃ ὁ ἀνήρ ἕως ἂν τελέσῃ τὸ ῥῆμα σήμερον
> Literal: not for not will-rest the man until he-finishes the matter today
> Natural: for the man will not rest until he has finished the matter today.

τελέω is the verb for bringing a task to its end, and Jesus says that his task has been brought there. (A papyrus citation in the Moulton–Milligan lexicon uses it for "until you accomplish this for me". That entry is OCR text and unverified.)

## Point 3

**"Once for all", Romans 6:10, where the text says it.**

If you want to preach that his death is not repeated, you don't need to squeeze it out of a tense. Paul says it with a word: ἐφάπαξ. `word ἐφάπαξ` glosses it "once / at once" and counts it 5 times in the NT. The in-verse gloss here is "once for all". The versions show that range: BSB "once for all", KJV "once", WEB "one time". The text says he died to sin ἐφάπαξ, and it sets that against a life he now lives "to God".

- Text says: one death, to sin; a life now lived to God.
- Within Paul's argument: the same verse sets this one death against a life he goes on living "to God".
- Leaves open: the grammar alone doesn't decide between "once" and "once for all". The verse's contrast between one death and a continuing life is the context to weigh.

## Conclusion

God's power in the good news is the power that leads people out, aimed at rescue for everyone who trusts. Jesus' word on the cross says it has been brought to its end and stands complete, with "everything" finished, as John puts it two verses earlier. And Paul tells us in a plain word that he died to sin ἐφάπαξ.

What these passages settle: the good news is God's power for rescue; Jesus said "it has been finished"; Paul says he died to sin ἐφάπαξ. What they leave open: whether ἐφάπαξ means "once" or "once for all" here, and how each tradition has built on these claims. For that we keep reading, with our Greek in hand.

"It has been finished."
