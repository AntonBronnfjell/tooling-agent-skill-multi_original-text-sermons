# The Tree by the Water — Psalm 1:1–3

## Introduction

Everyone wants a good life. Advertisers promise it, influencers sell it, and most of us spend a fair amount of energy chasing it. But what does a good life actually look like?

The book of Psalms opens with an answer. Its very first word is a cry: "Oh, how happy!" And then, in three verses, it paints a picture of the person it calls happy. Today we will read those three verses slowly, in Hebrew and in plain English, and learn two or three things about how to read Hebrew poetry along the way.

## Text

Psalm 1 stands at the door of the whole book of Psalms. It does not tell a story; it draws a contrast. Here are its first three verses:

> [HEB Ps.1.1] אַשְׁרֵי־הָאִישׁ אֲשֶׁר׀ לֹא הָלַךְ בַּעֲצַת רְשָׁעִים
> Literal: how-happy-[are] the-man who not walked in-counsel-of wicked-ones
> Natural: Oh, how happy is the one who has not walked by the advice of the wicked,

> [HEB Ps.1.1] וּבְדֶרֶךְ חַטָּאִים לֹא עָמָד וּבְמוֹשַׁב לֵצִים לֹא יָשָׁב
> Literal: and-in-way-of sinners not stood, and-in-seat-of mockers not sat
> Natural: has not stood on the road that wrongdoers take, and has not sat down where mockers sit.

> [HEB Ps.1.2] כִּי אִם בְּתוֹרַת יְהוָה חֶפְצוֹ וּבְתוֹרָתוֹ יֶהְגֶּה יוֹמָם וָלָיְלָה
> Literal: but rather in-instruction-of YHWH his-delight, and-in-his-instruction he-murmurs by-day and-night
> Natural: Instead, YHWH's instruction is what delights them; they murmur it over day and night.

> [HEB Ps.1.3] וְהָיָה כְּעֵץ שָׁתוּל עַל־פַּלְגֵי מָיִם אֲשֶׁר פִּרְיוֹ׀ יִתֵּן בְּעִתּוֹ
> Literal: and-he-is like-tree planted by-channels-of water, which its-fruit gives in-its-time
> Natural: They are like a tree planted by channels of water, that gives its fruit in its season,

> [HEB Ps.1.3] וְעָלֵהוּ לֹא־יִבּוֹל וְכֹל אֲשֶׁר־יַעֲשֶׂה יַצְלִיחַ
> Literal: and-its-leaf not withers, and-all that he-does he-makes-succeed / succeeds
> Natural: whose leaf does not wither; and whatever they do, they bring it to success / it succeeds.

A word on the divine name: the text has the four letters YHWH, which many English Bibles print as "the LORD". I write the letters. And one note on "the man": the Hebrew word is masculine, but the psalm describes a way of life, so in the natural line I use "the one", and then "they".

## Big Idea

The happy life is a rooted life: the one who delights in YHWH's instruction and keeps murmuring it is like a tree planted by water, fruitful in its season.

Three words carry the idea: אַשְׁרֵי, "how happy"; חֶפְצוֹ, "his delight"; and שָׁתוּל, "planted".

## Point 1

**What the happy one refuses (v. 1).**

Notice the three verbs: walked, stood, sat. And the three places: the counsel of the wicked, the road of sinners, the seat of mockers. It is tempting to preach a slide from walking to standing to sitting, and some do. But here is a reading lesson: Hebrew poetry works in parallel lines, saying a thing and then saying it again from another angle. We should not build a sermon on the difference between synonyms in parallel lines. Read the three lines together: this person does not take their direction from people who scorn God's way.

How do we know the verbs are "has not walked" and not "does not walk"? Our tool tags all three as qatal forms (Vqp3ms). The qatal often presents an action as a whole, and its time comes from context. The YLT says "Hath not walked"; the KJV says "walketh not". Both are fair.

A "how we know" moment about the last group, לֵצִים, "mockers". The old Greek translation, the Septuagint, has a different word there: λοιμῶν, "pests, pestilent ones". In Septuagint verses, λοιμός and this Hebrew word for mocking stand in the same verse six times (that is co-occurrence, not word-for-word alignment). It is a different picture of the same people: not only scoffers, but a plague.

And the first word, אַשְׁרֵי: our tool glosses it "how blessed!" It is an exclamation, not a command. The psalm is not saying "be blessed by doing this"; it is saying "look how well off this person is". "Happy" and "blessed" are both used for it; the Septuagint's word is μακάριος.

## Point 2

**What the happy one delights in (v. 2).**

"But rather": the psalm turns from what this person avoids to what they love. Their delight is תּוֹרָה. Many Bibles say "the law". The tool's lexicon gives "instruction"; its gloss in this verse is "law"; and in the Septuagint the word νόμος, "law", co-occurs with תּוֹרָה 188 times (same verse, not alignment). Both are fair renderings. I use "instruction" because the psalm speaks of something you delight in and chew on, which fits teaching as well as rules.

Now the verb: יֶהְגֶּה. Most Bibles say "meditates". But look at how the word is used elsewhere. Our tool glosses it "to mutter". In Isaiah 31:4 the same verb describes a lion growling over its prey. In Joshua 1:8, the instruction is not to "depart from your mouth" and you are to יֶהְגֶּה it day and night. So in the contexts we checked, this word involves the voice, not only silent thought. I chose "murmur it over" for this verse: reading under your breath, again and again, until it becomes part of you. Don't pour every sense into it, though: in Psalm 2:1 the same verb means "plotting". The context of Psalm 1 is delight.

And "day and night": not a slot in the schedule, but a companion through the day.

## Point 3

**What the happy one becomes (v. 3).**

"Like a tree planted." The word שָׁתוּל is a passive participle: planted, set there by someone. A tree does not plant itself by the channels of water. The life described here is received before it is achieved.

Look at what the tree does: it gives its fruit בְּעִתּוֹ, "in its season". Not every week. Fruit has a time. Some of you are in a season where nothing seems to grow; the psalm does not say a rooted life bears fruit constantly. It says the leaf does not wither.

Then the last line, which translations handle differently. The verb יַצְלִיחַ can be read two ways. It can mean "he makes it succeed": the same verb form in Joshua 1:8 has an object, "you will make your way succeed", and the YLT reads Psalm 1:3 this way: "all that he doth he causeth to prosper". Or it can mean "it succeeds, it prospers", which is how the tool glosses it here and how the KJV, BSB and WEB read it. The Septuagint uses a passive: "will be made to prosper". The text allows both; the subject could be the person, or the line could still be describing the tree. Either way, success here is the fruit of rootedness, not a promise of wealth.

## Conclusion

Psalm 1 answers our question about the good life. The happy one is not the one with the most, but the one who is rooted: refusing the counsel of mockers, delighting in YHWH's instruction, murmuring it day and night, and so growing like a tree by water.

Here is a practice for this week. Choose one verse of Psalm 1 each day and read it aloud, quietly, three times in the morning and once before sleep. That is what יֶהְגֶּה looks like in practice. At the end of the week, ask: what did I murmur over most this week, YHWH's instruction or someone else's advice?

"They are like a tree planted by channels of water, that gives its fruit in its season."
