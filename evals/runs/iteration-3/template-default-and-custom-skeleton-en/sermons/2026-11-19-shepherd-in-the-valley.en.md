# The Shepherd in the Valley — Psalm 23:1–4

## Introduction

Most of us could recite Psalm 23 half asleep. We have heard it at funerals, read it in hospital rooms, seen it framed on a grandmother's wall. That familiarity is a gift, but it can also become a fog: the words are so smooth that we stop asking what they actually say.

So today we are going to read these four verses slowly, in the Hebrew in which they were written, word by word, and then in plain English. The aim is that you leave able to read it yourself, and to notice the one small grammatical turn in verse 4 that carries the whole psalm.

## Text

Psalm 23 is a short song of trust, spoken in the first person by someone who pictures himself as a sheep and YHWH, Israel's God, as his shepherd. The first three verses talk about the shepherd; the fourth talks to him.

> [HEB Ps.23.1] יְהוָה רֹעִי לֹא אֶחְסָר
> Literal: YHWH [is] shepherding-me / my-shepherd; not I-lack
> Natural: YHWH is my shepherd; I lack nothing.

> [HEB Ps.23.2] בִּנְאוֹת דֶּשֶׁא יַרְבִּיצֵנִי עַל־מֵי מְנֻחוֹת יְנַהֲלֵנִי
> Literal: in-pastures-of grass he-makes-lie-down-me; by waters-of rests he-leads-me
> Natural: In grassy pastures he lets me lie down; to waters where I can rest he leads me.

> [HEB Ps.23.3] נַפְשִׁי יְשׁוֹבֵב יַנְחֵנִי בְמַעְגְּלֵי־צֶדֶק לְמַעַן שְׁמוֹ
> Literal: my-self/my-life he-brings-back; he-guides-me in-tracks-of right/rightness for-sake-of his-name
> Natural: He brings me back to life; he guides me along the right tracks for the sake of his name.

> [HEB Ps.23.4] גַּם כִּי־אֵלֵךְ בְּגֵיא צַלְמָוֶת לֹא־אִירָא רָע כִּי־אַתָּה עִמָּדִי
> Literal: even when I-walk in-valley-of deep-darkness / shadow-of-death, not I-fear harm, for you [are] with-me
> Natural: Even when I walk through a valley of deep darkness, I fear no harm, for you are with me.

> [HEB Ps.23.4] שִׁבְטְךָ וּמִשְׁעַנְתֶּךָ הֵמָּה יְנַחֲמֻנִי
> Literal: your-rod and-your-staff, they they-comfort-me
> Natural: your rod and your staff, they are what comfort me.

The divine name here is the four letters YHWH. I write them as they stand; many English Bibles print "the LORD" at this point, following an old reading custom. The one Dead Sea Scroll fragment in our data that has these verses agrees with the Hebrew text in every letter that survives; the old Greek translation differs mainly in its verb tenses, which we will look at in Point 1. The places where English translations part ways are a phrase in verse 3 and a word in verse 4, and we will look at them there.

## Big Idea

Because YHWH is my shepherd, I lack nothing: he gives rest, he brings me back and guides me, and in the darkest valley he is not only ahead of me but with me.

Two words of the text carry this idea. The first is רֹעִי, "my shepherd", which sets up the whole picture. The second is אַתָּה, "you", in verse 4, where the sheep stops talking about the shepherd and starts talking to him.

## Point 1

**The shepherd who leaves me lacking nothing (vv. 1–2).**

Look at the verb at the end of verse 1: אֶחְסָר. Our tool tags it as a yiqtol form, first person. Hebrew verb forms do not mark past, present and future the way English tenses do; the yiqtol often describes something incomplete, ongoing or expected. So the line can be heard as "I lack nothing" or "I will not lack". The KJV's "I shall not want" picks the future; the WEB's "I shall lack nothing" does too. Both are fair; the point is that the sheep's confidence covers the present and what is coming.

Here is a "how we know" moment. The old Greek translation of this psalm, the Septuagint, renders the line with a future verb and turns the noun "shepherd" into a verb: "the lord shepherds me". And in verses 2 and 3 it uses past-tense verbs where the Hebrew uses yiqtol forms. That is not a mistake; it shows that ancient translators also had to choose how to bring these Hebrew forms into another language. When you see different tenses in different Bibles, this is often why.

What does the shepherd provide? Grass to lie down in, and מְנֻחוֹת, literally "waters of rests". Notice the verbs: "he makes me lie down", "he leads me". The sheep does not find the pasture; it is brought there.

Application: many of us treat rest as something we earn after the work is done. The psalm pictures rest as something the shepherd gives. Lying down in his pasture is not laziness; it is trust.

## Point 2

**The shepherd who brings me back and keeps me on track (v. 3).**

Verse 3 opens with נַפְשִׁי. Many Bibles say "my soul". The tool's lexicon gives "soul", but its gloss in this verse is "self", and in Hebrew this word very often means the whole living person, "me", "my life". So "he restores my soul" is not about a spiritual part of me separate from my body. It is closer to "he brings me back to life", the way a tired, hungry sheep is revived. The verb, יְשׁוֹבֵב, comes from the common verb "to return"; the Septuagint renders it with "turned back".

Then: "he guides me בְמַעְגְּלֵי־צֶדֶק". The first word means tracks or paths, the ruts a cart or a flock wears into the ground. The second word, צֶדֶק, is usually translated "righteousness". Here the text allows two readings. It may mean "paths of righteousness", the right way to live. Or, staying inside the shepherd picture, "right tracks", paths that actually lead where they should. The Hebrew phrase can carry either, and I have kept "right tracks" in the translation and named the other option here.

And why does he do it? "For the sake of his name." The shepherd's reputation is tied to the sheep. A shepherd who lost his flock in the hills would be no shepherd at all.

Application: when you have wandered, the psalm does not say "find your way back". It says he brings you back.

## Point 3

**The shepherd who is with me in the valley (v. 4).**

Now the grammar turns. Verses 1 to 3 speak about YHWH: "he makes me lie down", "he leads me", "he guides me". Verse 4 suddenly speaks to him: "for you are with me". The pronoun אַתָּה, "you", stands right there in the line. In the darkest place of the psalm, the sheep stops describing the shepherd and starts addressing him. That is the turn I promised you, and it is something you can watch for in other psalms too: who is being talked about, and who is being talked to?

What is the valley? The word is צַלְמָוֶת, and here translations honestly differ. One option is "deep darkness": that is how our tool glosses it in this verse, and the same word elsewhere names a darkness that YHWH turns into morning (Amos 5:8). The other option is "shadow of death": the Septuagint renders it with two words meaning "shadow of death", and the KJV, BSB and WEB follow that. In Job 10:21 it describes the land from which one goes and does not return, so death is not far from it either. The text allows both; either way, the valley is the place where you cannot see.

And in that place: "I fear no harm, for you are with me." Then the rod and the staff, two shepherd's tools; the tool glosses the first "staff" (the same word can also mean "tribe") and the second "staff". The sheep says they "comfort" it. The tool's gloss for the verb is "to be sorry: comfort"; it is the same verb that opens Isaiah 40:1, "comfort, comfort my people".

Application: the psalm does not promise there is no valley. It promises a companion in it.

## Conclusion

Psalm 23 is not a poem about a pleasant countryside. It is a confession of trust: YHWH is my shepherd, so I lack nothing; he gives rest, he brings me back, and when the path goes down into darkness, he is with me.

Here is a practice for this week. Read Psalm 23:1–4 aloud each morning. On Monday, notice every "he". On Tuesday, notice the one "you". By the end of the week, try praying verse 4 in the second person in your own valley, whatever it is: not "God is with me" as a statement about him, but "you are with me" as words spoken to him.

"Even when I walk through a valley of deep darkness, I fear no harm, for you are with me."
